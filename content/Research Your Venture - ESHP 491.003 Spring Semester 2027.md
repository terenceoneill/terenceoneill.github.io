This course is designed for a 10 week, 80 minute class schedule. The design purposefully is interactive and social, and encourages people to connect ideas across source types. This course outline is the product of several credited courses I have taught over the past 12 years, many rounds of iteration, and some helpful co-work using AI. If you have any questions, feel free to reach out. 
-Terence O'Neill, November 19, 2026

# Researching Your Venture (RYV) — 10-Week Course Outline

**Format:** 80-minute sessions, once weekly  
**Term:** Spring 2027  
**Audience:** Nascent entrepreneurs, any business stage; no prior business coursework required

---
## Week 1: Getting Started — Values, Problems, and Your Model of Success
### Learning Objectives
- Articulate personal definition of entrepreneurial success (Bloom: Create)
- Identify problems worth solving in their immediate environment (Bloom: Understand)
- Connect their values to venture decisions they'll make over the semester (Bloom: Connect)
### What Students Produce
- **5-Year Vision** (written): Picture of successful business + lifestyle in 5 years. What do you do on a day-to-day basis? What kind of venture are you building? (artifact: handwritten or typed, 15 min to create)
- **Bug List** (physical, on campus): Walk the library or campus, notice problems/friction points/gaps, list 3–5 things worth solving (artifact: photo of list or typed notes, 20 min to create)
- **Verbal share-out** (classroom): Pair or small-group share on one of the above
### Source Materials & Frameworks
- 5-year vision prompt (instructor-provided)
- Physical space (library or campus)
- No databases or readings required this week - Syllabus reading due
### Standards for Success
- 5-year vision is specific enough that you could describe it to a friend and they'd understand what winning looks like for you
- Bug List contains observable problems in a real space (not theoretical)
- Student can articulate why one of these problems is worth their time
### Connection to Next Week
Moves from "what matters to me" to "how do I structure a business around what I see?" Week 2 builds the Business Model Canvas as a tool to organize thinking about the venture you're considering.

---
## Week 2: What's a Business? — Business Model Canvas and Market Context
### Learning Objectives
- Map the internal structure of a business (Bloom: Understand)
- Identify key success factors for launching a business (Bloom: Analyze)
- Recognize how market dynamics reshape business model decisions (Bloom: Analyze)
### What Students Produce
- **Business Model Canvas** (hand-drawn or digital template): Full BMC for your venture idea
- **Market snapshot from IBISWorld** (notes or screenshot): One key statistic about your industry + what it tells you about how to structure your business (artifact: 1 paragraph, 15 min to create)
- **Team presentation** (in class): Present your BMC to another team, connect it to one market insight
### Source Materials & Frameworks
- Business Model Canvas (template + examples provided)
- IBISWorld (library database; instructor walks students through account login and basic search)
- BMC lecture/examples (live, in-class)
### Standards for Success
- BMC is populated in all 9 sections (even if some are "still figuring it out")
- At least one section shifts based on what IBISWorld told you (show the "before" and "after" thinking)
- You can explain why one BMC section matters for your specific business (not generic)
### Connection to Next Week
You've named your business model. Now: how do you know if your assumptions about the market are right? Week 3 teaches critical thinking about information sources, especially AI—a tool you'll use throughout the research.

---
## Week 3: AI and Information Literacy — SIFT for AI Claims
### Learning Objectives
- Evaluate the source and credibility of AI-generated claims (Bloom: Evaluate)
- Trace a claim backward from AI summary to original research (Bloom: Analyze)
- Identify gaps between what AI "knows" and what matters for your business (Bloom: Evaluate)
### What Students Produce
- **Claim trace** (written, 1–2 pages): Pick one claim about your business/industry from an AI tool (ChatGPT, Copilot, etc.). Apply SIFT (Stop, Investigate source, Find better versions, Trace to original). Write up:
    - The original AI claim
    - Where you investigated it (what source? what did it say?)
    - What surprised you or what's missing
    - Artifact: photo of handwritten work or typed document, 20 min to create
### Source Materials & Frameworks
- SIFT for AI framework (Mike Caulfield's article/blog; link provided)
- One AI tool of student's choice (ChatGPT, Copilot, Google Gemini, etc.)
- Scientific research, trade press, or industry reports (students source based on their claim)
- Reading: How AI works
### Standards for Success 
- You traced the claim to at least one original source (not just "the AI said it")
- You found a gap between what AI claimed and what the original source actually says
- You can name one question the AI couldn't answer about your business
### Connection to Next Week
You now know how to interrogate information. Week 4 teaches market sizing—a core research skill where you'll practice finding and combining data from multiple sources.

---
## Week 4: Market Sizing — TAM, SAM, SOM and Bottom-Up Research
### Learning Objectives
- Understand the three market definitions and why they matter (Bloom: Understand)
- Build a market-sizing model from real data (Bloom: Apply)
- Critique market estimates and identify sources of uncertainty (Bloom: Evaluate)
### What Students Produce
- **Market Sizing Model** (spreadsheet or written): Calculate or estimate TAM, SAM, and SOM for your business using 2–3 different approaches
    - Top-down (industry data from IBISWorld, Statista, or similar)
    - Bottom-up (unit economics: price × target customer count)
    - Sanity check against a comparable market or company
- **Source documentation**: Name your sources for each number; note where data is missing or uncertain
- Artifact: screenshot or PDF of model, 25 min to create
### Source Materials & Frameworks
- TAM/SAM/SOM definitions (lecture + worked example provided)
- Three professional databases: IBISWorld, Statista, Mintel (library access provided; instructor walks through each)
- Optional: Comparable company analysis (find one similar business, extract public financial data if available)
### Standards for Success
- Each number in your TAM/SAM/SOM is traceable to a source (not guessed)
- You can explain _why_ you chose each approach (top-down vs. bottom-up) and what it revealed
- You've identified at least one piece of data you couldn't find (and written what you'd need to know instead)
### Connection to Next Week
You've sized the market. Now: who exactly are your customers, and what do they need? Week 5 teaches customer research through interviews—primary research that fills gaps your databases can't.

---
## Week 5: Customer Research — Interviewing and DMU Roles
### Learning Objectives
- Understand the roles in a customer's purchase decision (Decision-Making Unit: End User, Champion, Economic Buyer, etc.) (Bloom: Understand)
- Design an interview guide that probes for real customer needs (Bloom: Create)
- Practice interviewing across different customer roles (Bloom: Apply)
- Apply the epistemic ritual "Guess first, then verify, then name the gap" to customer research (Bloom: Apply)
### What Students Produce
- **The "I Assume..." Flip** (written, 5 min in class): Write three sentences starting "I assume my customer..." and flip each into a research question. These questions become your interview agenda. _(Ritual from IS4E)_
- **Interview guide** (written, 1–2 pages): 8–10 open-ended questions designed to understand what jobs your customer is trying to do and who influences the decision. Draw on your flipped assumptions as starting points.
- **Role-play interviews** (in class, recorded or notes): Students pair up and role-play as different DMU roles; conduct mock interviews with each other
- **Interview debrief notes**: What surprised you? What would you ask differently next time? Where did your assumptions hold up vs. where did you learn something new?
- Artifact: typed "I Assume..." flip + interview guide + photo/notes from role-play, 30 min to create
### Source Materials & Frameworks
- Jobs to be Done concept (Aulet, _Disciplined Entrepreneurship_, Ch. 1 excerpt, or instructor summary)
- DMU roles framework: End User, Champion, Economic Buyer, Technical Evaluator, Influencer (definitions + examples provided)
- IDEO Design Kit interview tips (linked or excerpted) OR SAGE Methods
- "I Assume... Flip" epistemic ritual (instructor demonstration)
### Standards for Success
- Your interview guide asks _why_ customers choose (or don't choose) solutions, not just _what_ they want
- You can name the DMU role you're interviewing and explain why that role matters to your decision
- You've practiced listening without solving (in the role-play, you ask follow-up questions instead of pitching)
### Deliverable Due: **Brief A1** (end of Week 5)
- Synthesis of customer interviews + market sizing completed in Weeks 4–5
- Rubric and format TBD
### Connection to Next Week
Interviews give you rich, specific customer stories. Week 6 teaches customer mapping—turning those stories into a visual picture of who your customer is at scale.

---
## Week 6: Customer Mapping — Demographics, Psychographics, and Data Bias
### Learning Objectives
- Identify observable characteristics and values that segment your customer (Bloom: Understand)
- Map customers geographically and demographically using real data (Bloom: Apply)
- Recognize how data collection methods introduce bias (Bloom: Evaluate)
### What Students Produce
- **Customer profile** (written + visual): Demographic snapshot (age, income, location, role) + psychographic sketch (values, frustrations, aspirations)
- **Map** (SimplyMap or hand-drawn): Show where your target customer lives or works; include at least one demographic layer (population density, income, education)
- **Bias reflection** (1 paragraph): What data is _not_ available? What assumptions did you make? Where might census or commercial data mislead you?
- Artifact: typed profile + screenshot or photo of map + reflection, 30 min to create
### Source Materials & Frameworks
- U.S. Census Bureau (American Community Survey / ACS data)
- SimplyMap (library database; instructor demonstrates account setup and basic map-building)
- Demographic dataset dictionaries (provided)
- Bias awareness discussion (in-class, prompted by instructor: "What did the census miss about your customer?")
- Build off of ODI work done in StoryTelling Class
### Standards for Success
- Your map is built from real data, not guesswork (you cite the source)
- You've noticed at least one gap between the census portrait and the customer you interviewed
- You can articulate one way a business decision might differ if you relied only on this map
### Connection to Next Week
You've mapped your customer at scale. Now: what's changing in the world that affects them? Week 7 teaches environmental scanning—trends, regulation, competitive moves—through PESTLE and Power Searching.

---
## Week 7: Environmental Scanning — PESTLE, Trends, and Power Searching
### Learning Objectives
- Distinguish between facts, fads, and trends (Bloom: Understand)
- Map political, economic, social, technological, legal, and environmental forces (Bloom: Analyze)
- Source and evaluate credible trend signals (Bloom: Evaluate)
### What Students Produce
- **Trend Hunting Grid** (written, 1 page): Identify 3 trends affecting your business—at least 1 demand-side (customer behavior, channels) and 1 supply-side (operations, tech, regulation, costs). For each:
    - Trend name + description
    - Why it matters for your business
    - At least 2 sources (trade press, government, competitor, technologists, etc.)
- **PESTLE scan** (written, 1–2 pages): For each of the six categories, name one signal (or "none relevant") and its source
- **Power Search artifact** (screenshot): Show one successful deep-dive search (e.g., find a trade association's position paper on regulation; use site:, filetype:pdf, etc.)
- Artifact: typed grid + PESTLE + screenshot, 30 min to create
### Source Materials & Frameworks
- PESTLE definitions + worked example (provided)
- Fact/Fad/Trend distinction (lecture + discussion; examples from student ventures)
- Power Searching operators: site:, filetype:, exact phrase, OR (live demo in class)
- Google Trends (live demo with student ventures as search terms)
- Source categories: trade associations (via IBISWorld), government agencies (.gov), journalists, technologists, industry conferences, customer signals
### Standards for Success
- Each trend is named as a complete sentence, not a topic ("Younger customers are shifting to DTC brands" not "DTC shift")
- You can explain _why_ this trend matters for your business (not generic)
- Your Power Search found a credible source (not a blog; something original)
### Deliverable Due: **Brief A2** (end of Week 7 or beginning of Week 8)
- Synthesis of customer research + trends + market context
- [Rubric and format TBD]
### Connection to Next Week
You've scanned the environment. Now: how do you turn all this information into usable insights? Week 8 teaches prompt engineering—how to feed your research _into_ AI and interrogate what comes back.

## Week 8: Synthesis and Prompt Engineering — CRAFT Framework
### Learning Objectives
- Structure a clear prompt that feeds research findings into AI (Bloom: Create)
- Interrogate AI output against your original research (Bloom: Evaluate)
- Identify where AI invents vs. where it synthesizes (Bloom: Analyze)
### What Students Produce
- **CRAFT Prompt** (written): Design one prompt using the CRAFT framework (Context, Role, Ask, Format, Tone) that feeds your customer research + market data into an AI tool
    - Example: "You are [role]. Here's what I learned about my customer [context]. Please [ask] in [format] using [tone]."
- **AI Output** (screenshot or pasted): Run the prompt; capture the result
- **Interrogation notes** (1–2 pages):
    - What did the AI synthesize well? (where did it connect your research?)
    - What did it invent? (where did it go beyond your data?)
    - What's still unanswered?
- Artifact: typed prompt + screenshot + interrogation notes, 25 min to create
### Source Materials & Frameworks
- CRAFT prompt engineering framework (lecture + examples; link to Mintel/Statista worked examples)
- One AI tool (ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini—student's choice)
- Comparison table: "What LLMs are good at / bad at" (instructor-provided; includes: personas, cross-domain synthesis vs. limited human experience, recency limits, domain legibility)
### Standards for Success
- Your prompt is specific enough that someone else could run it and get similar output
- You found at least one place where AI overstepped (claimed something you didn't research or embellished)
- You used AI to _synthesize_ your findings, not to _replace_ your research
### Connection to Next Week

You've synthesized across multiple sources. Week 9 teaches signal processing—organizing all the information you've collected so you can track what matters and what's changed.

---
## Week 9: Signals Processing — Building a Personal Information System

### Learning Objectives
- Distinguish between ambient signals (passive noticing) and deliberate signals (active research) (Bloom: Understand)
- Design a filtering system for information relevant to your venture (Bloom: Create)
- Practice building a knowledge management habit (Bloom: Apply)
- Adopt the "Capture, Store, Connect" ritual as a sustainable practice (Bloom: Apply)
### What Students Produce
- **Signals collection** (manual, physical or digital): Gather 15–20 signals from the past week related to your business (headlines, customer comments, competitor moves, regulatory news, technology announcements, etc.)
- **Filtering criteria** (written, 1 page): Define what counts as a signal for _your_ venture:
    - Does it affect customer behavior? Market size? Regulation? Competitors?
    - Is it a one-off or a pattern?
    - How will you know if you should act on it?
- **System design sketch** (written or drawn): How will you capture, store, and review signals going forward? _(Following the Capture-Store-Connect ritual from IS4E)_
    - What tool? (notebook, spreadsheet, email folder, bookmark system, etc.)
    - How often do you review?
    - What's the one question you ask of each signal?
    - How will you _connect_ new signals to prior work? (one note linking it to your earlier research, one pattern you're tracking, etc.)
- Artifact: typed or photographed collection + criteria + system sketch, 20 min to create
### Source Materials & Frameworks
- "Signals vs. noise" definition (instructor-provided)
- Knowledge Management integration ritual: **Capture, Store, Connect** (from IS4E; appears throughout Weeks 1–9)
    - **Capture**: photograph/digitize what you produced this week
    - **Store**: place it in your knowledge management system
    - **Connect**: write one note linking it to your other work
- No databases required; students source signals from their own reading/observation
### Standards for Success
- Your filtering criteria is specific to your business (not generic)
- You've designed a system you could actually maintain (not elaborate/unrealistic)
- You can explain why tracking signals matters for a venture at your stage
### Connection to Week 10
You've built a system for ongoing learning. Week 10 is the capstone: present your venture evolution and consult the Norns—an AI-assisted reflection on what changed and what still matters.

## Week 10: Capstone — Final Presentations and Consulting the Norns
### Learning Objectives
- Articulate how your thinking changed over the semester (Bloom: Analyze)
- Synthesize learning across customers, market, trends, and signals (Bloom: Synthesize)
- Forecast future information needs (Bloom: Evaluate)
- Reflect on where your venture requires artisanal commitment vs. exponential scaling (Bloom: Analyze)
### What Students Produce
**Format depends on class size:**
#### Path A: Smaller class (5–10 students)
- **Final presentation** (5–7 min, no slides): Tell the story of your venture evolution
    - Open with a quote or moment from Brief 1
    - What assumption did you have to revise?
    - What one market insight shifted your business model?
    - Close: What's the next question you need to answer?
- **Two Columns reflection** (written or drawn, 5 min in class): _(From IS4E Class 12)_
    - Draw two columns: **Exponential** and **Artisanal**
    - First pass: For your venture, what scales through tools/systems? What must be built by hand?
    - Second pass: For _yourself_ as an entrepreneur, what do you practice like a craft (not for a grade but because it compounds)? Where are you pursuing exponential gains?
    - Artifact: photo of drawn reflection or typed version
- **Consulting the Norns** (written, online form, 2 rounds):
    - Round 1: Three questions mapped to Norse mythology
        - **Urd** ("What Was"): One assumption you've revised this semester. Why?
        - **Verdandi** ("What Is Becoming"): Where is AI changing what an entrepreneur needs to know?
        - **Skuld** ("What Shall Be"): What information skill is most underestimated five years from now?
    - Instructor synthesizes responses (collect, synthesize, interrogate with AI; share back as "The Mirror")
    - Round 2: Same questions, answered from an imagined vantage of 2031
    - Debrief: What changed between Round 1 and Round 2, and when did you notice it changing?
#### Path B: Larger class (10+ students)
- Same final presentation (time-boxed)
- Two Columns reflection (shortened, ~3 min)
- Consulting the Norns (same 2-round structure)
- All three activities complete in the same 80-min session
### Source Materials & Frameworks
- Norns mythology frame (Urd/Verdandi/Skuld; brief explanation provided)
- Modified Delphi method (instructor explains: collect individual responses → synthesize → return for reflection)
- Exponential vs. Artisanal distinction (worked example: instructor discusses personal mushroom cultivation vs. tool-building; students apply to their venture and themselves)
- Briefs 1–2 (students review their own writing as reference for stories)
### Standards for Success (Wong Test)
- Presentation shows a real change in thinking (not a summary of research findings)
- You can name the source of your insight (an interview, a database finding, a trend, etc.)
- Round 2 responses show you reconsidered your earlier answers (not identical to Round 1)

## Principles Underlying This Outline

- **Artifact-driven**: Every session produces something students can see and revise; nothing lives in their heads alone
- **Wong standard**: Clear deliverables; repeatable expectations; can be taught by someone else using this outline
- **Aulet standard**: Students build evaluative frameworks, not just data collection; they learn to ask _better questions_, not just find answers
- **Physical engagement**: Mix of oral, written, visual, and hands-on (Bug List, interviews, mapping, Power Searching); work at the level of story and imagery (totems, symbols) when possible
- **Synthesis emphasis**: Later weeks build on earlier research; synthesis _across_ sources is a learnable skill
- **Epistemic rituals**: Recurring moves that become habits—"I Assume... Flip" (Week 5), "Guess first, then verify, then name the gap" (throughout), "Capture, Store, Connect" (recurring KM ritual)
- **AI literacy**: Not fear of AI; understanding what it can and can't do, then using it strategically
- **Information Synthesis as core skill**: No single source contains the answer; contradictions between sources are productive; building useful models of reality from heterogeneous inputs is learnable
